Fairness II: causal

Class

Author

Joshua Loftus

Note

This is the student version of the class sheet. The exercises are for your own time; the class works through everything under “In class”. Answers are revealed in class.

How this sheet works

Exercises rederive or apply the lecture’s results. Do them on your own time; they are not worked in class. The class works the items under “In class,” in that order, then what it can of “If there is time.” Discussions are in groups of three or four, then with the room. The problem is gone through as soon as the room has tried it.

The first half is about causal structure: what a model lets you hold fixed, and what changes when the graph changes. The second half is about the judgments the structure does not make: which framework, which criterion, which paths are fair, and what to do about luck. Every decision here prices or ranks people, and the fairness criteria give different prices from the same data. So each item ends by asking who the error falls on, who gains from the number that was reported, or who the choice could be justified to. The model narrows the answer; it does not give it.

Notation as in the lecture. \(A\) is the attribute, \(M\) a mediator, \(Y\) the outcome, and \(U\) terms are what the model leaves unobserved. \(Y(a, m)\) is the outcome under \(do(A := a, M := m)\) and \(M(a)\) the mediator under \(do(A := a)\). \(\text{CDE}(m)\) is the controlled direct effect, \(E[Y(1, m) - Y(0, m)]\), with the mediator set to \(m\) for everyone. \(\text{NDE}_a\) is the natural direct effect with the mediator held at \(M(a)\), and \(\text{NIE}_a\) the natural indirect effect with \(A\) held at \(a\); without an interaction the subscripts can be dropped. Which value of \(A\) is 1 is said in each item.

Exercises

Attempt each part before you open a hint.

WarningHints

Do not open a hint unless you have been stuck for at least a few minutes, and open them in order.

Exercise 1. A contextual offer

A university predicts each applicant’s degree result \(Y\) from their school grade \(X\). Some applicants went to schools with fewer resources (\(A = 1\)), half of all applicants, and those schools lower a grade by one unit:

\[ X = U - A, \qquad Y = U + \varepsilon , \]

where \(U\) is what the applicant brings, with mean 0 and variance 1, independent of \(A\), and \(\varepsilon\) is independent noise with mean 0.

  1. Draw the graph. Which variables are descendants of \(A\)?

  2. Find the least-squares prediction of \(Y\) from \(X\) alone. What is its average in each group, and what is each group’s average \(Y\)?

  3. An applicant with \(A = 1\) has \(X = 1.5\). Find their prediction. Then compute the prediction had their school not lowered the grade. Is the prediction from \(X\) alone counterfactually fair?

  4. Find a counterfactually fair prediction with the same accuracy as the best prediction from \(X\) and \(A\). Does it use \(A\)?

  5. A contextual offer adds one unit to the grade of every applicant with \(A = 1\). Which prediction is that? Suppose that in fact some of those schools lower grades by two units and others by none. Who bears the error of a rule that adds one unit to all of them?

TipHint 1

For (b): the slope is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/\operatorname{Var}(X)\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(A) = 1/4\).

TipHint 2

For (c): abduction recovers \(U = X + A\).

Exercise 2. A pay gap

An employer’s pay data cover women (\(A = 1\)) and men in the same jobs. Years of experience \(M\) and annual pay \(Y\), in thousands of pounds, follow

\[ M = 10 - 2A + U_M, \qquad Y = 20 - A + M + U_Y , \]

with \(U_M\) and \(U_Y\) of mean 0, independent of \(A\) and of each other.

  1. Find \(\text{CDE}(m)\), the NDE, the NIE and the TE.

  2. The raw gap is \(E[Y \mid A = 1] - E[Y \mid A = 0]\). Which of the four effects does it estimate here, and why?

  3. A “like-for-like” comparison regresses \(Y\) on \(A\) and \(M\). Which effect does its coefficient on \(A\) estimate here? Name one thing about the world that would make it estimate something else.

  4. The employer reports the like-for-like gap, and a union reports the raw gap. Which number answers “is there discrimination?”, and who gains from each being the one reported?

TipHint 1

The model is the lecture’s linear model with \(\alpha = -2\), \(\beta = 1\) and \(\gamma = -1\).

Exercise 3. A lender’s two fair predictions

A lender predicts the expected annual loss \(Y\) on a loan, in hundreds of pounds, from years of credit history \(M\). Applicants under 25 have \(A = 1\), and are half the applicants:

\[ M = 8 - 4A + U_M, \qquad Y = 3 + 0.5A - 0.25M + U_Y , \]

with \(U_M\) and \(U_Y\) of mean 0, independent of \(A\) and of each other.

  1. Find the NDE, the NIE and the TE.

  2. Write down the full prediction \(E[Y \mid A, M]\), and its average for each group.

  3. The lender judges the path through credit history fair and the direct path unfair. Write down the path-specific prediction, and its average for each group.

  4. Write down the counterfactually fair prediction that prices every applicant as the full model would price them had they been 25 or over, and its average for each group.

  5. Shift each prediction by a constant so that it collects the average loss. What does each group pay under each?

  6. The lender estimates \(\alpha\) from its own past customers, who are applicants it approved, and gets \(\hat\alpha = -2\). It plugs this into (d). What does each group pay now, before the shift in (e)? Is the result counterfactually fair? Which group pays for the error in \(\hat\alpha\), and who could check it?

TipHint 1

For (d): abduction gives \(U_M = M - 8 + 4A\); set \(A := 0\) along every path.

TipHint 2

For (e): the average loss is the average of the two groups’ averages in (b).

Exercise 4. Dropping the attribute and refitting (harder)

The lender of exercise 3 may not use age, so it regresses \(Y\) on \(M\) alone. Take \(\operatorname{Var}(U_M) = 12\).

  1. Find \(\operatorname{Var}(A)\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(A, M)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(M)\).

  2. Find the slope of the regression, and the gap between the two groups’ average predictions.

  3. Show that the gap is \(\text{NIE} + R^2\,\text{NDE}\), where \(R^2\) is the share of the variance of \(M\) explained by \(A\).

  4. The rule “do not use age” is followed, and part of the direct effect of age returns through credit history. Who could notice, and who pays for it?

TipHint 1

\(\operatorname{Cov}(Y, M) = \gamma \operatorname{Cov}(A, M) + \beta \operatorname{Var}(M)\).

In class

Discussion 1: warm-up

You were asked to bring one decision in which an attribute is used, or deliberately not used, with one route by which it could affect the outcome, and whether that route is fair. If you did not bring one, choose: a car insurer’s premium; a bank’s credit limit; a university’s offer; a hiring panel’s shortlist.

  1. Draw the decision as a graph: the attribute, the route you brought and anything else that affects the outcome. Which arrows does the decision use?
  2. For the route you judged fair, describe one way in which the mediator itself could have been shaped unfairly.
  3. Who gets to say that the route is fair: the person decided about, the decision maker, or a regulator?

From memory: what to hold fixed

Without the notes, fill in the table: should an analysis hold the variable fixed, by conditioning or adjusting for it?

to estimate a total effect of \(A\) to estimate a controlled direct effect of \(A\) to predict \(Y\)
a confounder of \(A\) and \(Y\)
a mediator between \(A\) and \(Y\)
a collider of \(A\) and \(Y\)

A firm says: “We controlled for hours and occupation, and the pay gap between women and men vanished.” Which cell is that? What has it not shown, and who gains from the sentence?

Problem 1. A pay gap with an interaction

In another employer’s data, women (\(A = 1\)) also earn less per year of experience:

\[ M = 10 - 2A + U_M, \qquad Y = 20 - A + M - 0.2\,A M + U_Y , \]

in years and thousands of pounds, with \(U_M\) and \(U_Y\) of mean 0, independent of \(A\) and of each other.

  1. Find the TE.

  2. Find \(\text{NDE}_0\), the direct effect with experience held at the men’s level, and \(\text{NIE}_1\). What do they add up to?

  3. Find \(\text{NDE}_1\) and \(\text{NIE}_0\). What do they add up to?

  4. Someone reports \(\text{NDE}_0\) and \(\text{NIE}_0\) side by side. What do they add up to, and why is it not the TE?

  5. The employer reports a direct effect of \(-2.6\) and the union one of \(-3\). Are both correct? Who chooses the reference world, and who could the choice be justified to?

Discussion 2: is the graph right?

“All models are wrong but some are useful.” (Box, 1979)

Take the lecture’s mileage example: women (\(A = 1\)), mileage \(M\) in thousands of miles a year, claims \(Y\) in hundreds of pounds. Two analysts have the same records of gender, mileage and claims. The first draws the lecture’s graph and fits it to the records by least squares. The second supposes that the graph is missing \(Z\), a score for how much a person drives for work, which the insurer does not record. Each unit of \(Z\) adds \(4{,}000\) miles a year and, beyond what those miles add, £100 of claims. The second row is the world as it would be if the second analyst is right:

direct effect through mileage total
first analyst, fitted (no \(Z\)) \(-0.07\) \(-1.43\) \(-1.5\)
second analyst, supposed (with \(Z\)) \(-0.5\) \(-1.0\) \(-1.5\)
  1. Under each row, find the path-specific premium, removing the direct path, and the counterfactually fair premium, taken to be the least-squares premium on \(M + 4A\), the mileage a driver would have had as a man. How far apart are women’s and men’s average premiums under each? Which differ between the rows, and which do not?
  2. What evidence could tell the two graphs apart? Do the insurer’s records? The same question for problem 1: with only gender, experience and pay, can the employer’s data tell a direct effect from a variable nobody measured that affects both experience and pay?
  3. A regulator has to approve one pricing rule this year. Choose: wait for better evidence; adopt one analyst’s graph; or require the counterfactually fair premium. Defend your choice against the strongest objection to it. Who must the decision be justified to, and who pays if the graph behind it is wrong?

Discussion 3: a framework and a criterion

Each group takes one framework: consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics or pragmatic ethics.

  1. Which fairness criterion does your framework motivate: a price that does not use the attribute; a criterion on the table of who was selected and what happened (equal rates, calibration); a counterfactually fair price; a path-specific one? Make the best case.
  2. In the red car world, the price from the car alone is not counterfactually fair, and the counterfactually fair price that depends on the car uses gender. ms4ds suggests “there may be a deontological argument in favor of functional independence,” a process that does not take the attribute as an input. Does your framework side with the deontological argument or with counterfactual fairness here? Find a case where your framework and your criterion come apart.
  3. Who could each criterion be justified to: a careful woman driver who bought a red car, an average man, the insurer, a court?

Discussion 4: luck

  1. Give examples of things outside a person’s control (or that they can only weakly influence) that strongly impact their life.
  2. Pick one that a price, a loan or a hiring decision could use, or use something downstream of it. Is it brute luck or option luck? Should the decision ignore it?
  3. A rule that ignores it, collecting the same amount in total, moves cost to someone else. Who? Could you justify it to them?

Coding: two analysts, simulated

Base R is enough. The lecture’s mileage example with \(Z\), the score for how much a person drives for work from discussion 2, drawn from a normal distribution with mean \(1/4\) and variance \(3/16\). Mileage is in thousands of miles a year and claims in hundreds of pounds. Each driver has two potential mileages, as a man and as a woman.

n <- 100000
A <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)                  # 1 for a woman
Z <- rnorm(n, 0.25, sqrt(0.1875))       # how much the driver drives for work
U_M <- rnorm(n, 0, 2)
U_Y <- rnorm(n, 0, 1)
M0 <- 9 + 4 * Z + U_M                   # mileage had the driver been a man
M1 <- M0 - 4                            # mileage had the driver been a woman
M <- M0 - 4 * A                         # the mileage recorded
Y <- -0.5 * A + 0.25 * M + Z + U_Y      # the claims recorded
Y_0_M0 <- -0.5 * 0 + 0.25 * M0 + Z + U_Y
Y_1_M1 <- # ...
Y_1_M0 <- # ...
TE  <- mean(Y_1_M1 - Y_0_M0)
NDE <- # ...
NIE <- # ...
c(TE = TE, NDE = NDE, NIE = NIE)
coef(lm(Y ~ A + M))
coef(lm(Y ~ A + M + Z))
  1. Complete the three lines and compute the TE, NDE and NIE. Does the direct effect differ between drivers? Why?
  2. Run the two regressions. Which coefficient on \(A\) is the direct effect, and which is the first analyst’s from discussion 2?
  3. An auditor has only \(A\), \(M\) and \(Y\). Which of the numbers above could they compute, and which would they get wrong? Who gains if the auditor reports the first regression’s coefficient as the direct effect?
