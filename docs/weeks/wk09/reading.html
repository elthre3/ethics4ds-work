AI: a survey of the evidence

Reading

Benchmark validity

Bean et al. (2025) reviewed 445 benchmarks for large language models, drawn from 46,114 papers at leading machine learning and natural language processing conferences, and asked whether each one supports the claims made with its scores. Of the 445, 78.2% define the phenomenon they say they measure, but 47.8% of those definitions are contested. Fewer than 10% use complete real-world tasks, 53.4% present evidence that the score measures the intended construct, and 32.4% compare models with a human baseline. In 21.1%, at least part of the scoring requires answers in a structured format such as JSON (8.6% throughout), so the score also depends on whether a model follows the format. The review codes what each paper reports, so a missing justification does not show that a benchmark is invalid, and agreement between coders on the 46 papers coded twice was moderate on average (mean Brennan–Prediger kappa 0.524) and close to chance on whether the phenomenon was defined (0.073).

Construct validity, the social sciences’ term for whether a measurement captures the concept it names, has been applied to measures of fairness (Jacobs and Wallach, 2021) and to evaluations of generative AI (Wallach et al., 2025; with authors at Microsoft Research). Raji et al. (2021) argue that a few benchmarks are treated as measures of general progress, which a finite set of tasks cannot support. Rebuilding the ImageNet test set by the original procedure lowered accuracy by 11 to 14 points but left the order of models almost unchanged (Recht et al., 2019), and much of the drop came from how the new images were selected (Engstrom et al., 2020). Test labels contain errors (Northcutt et al., 2021), and grading that gives no credit for abstaining rewards guessing (Kalai et al., 2025; three of the four authors are at OpenAI).

Summary: Nearly half of the benchmark papers reviewed report no evidence that the benchmark measures what it is named for, and how test items are chosen, labeled and scored can change what a score means.

Uncertainty in evaluations and leaderboards

Singh et al. (2025) audited Chatbot Arena, a public leaderboard that ranks chatbots by crowd votes between pairs of anonymous answers, using about 2 million votes on 243 models from 42 providers between January 2024 and April 2025. A few providers tested many private versions of their models before a release, 27 from Meta in one month before Llama 4, and could choose which results to make public. Google and OpenAI received an estimated 19.2% and 20.4% of all data from the arena, inferred from their models’ shares of battles, and proprietary providers 61.4% in total, data that can be used to tune models to the leaderboard’s prompts. Of the 243 public models, 205 were silently deprecated, sampled at rates near zero without notice, against 47 listed as deprecated; 64% of the 205 were open models. Several of the authors work at Cohere, a company that competes on the leaderboard, as the paper discloses.

Few benchmarks use uncertainty estimates or statistical tests to compare results: 16.0% in Bean et al.’s (2025) review. Miller (2024; the author works at Anthropic) sets out standard errors, clustered errors for related questions and paired comparisons for evaluations. Intervals from the central limit theorem can be too narrow for benchmarks with fewer than a few hundred items (Bowyer et al., 2025). Many comparisons in natural language processing are underpowered (Card et al., 2020). Scores also move when the task does not change: reformatting a prompt without changing its meaning moved accuracy by up to 76 points for one model (Sclar et al., 2024), and reordering answer options moved models up to eight places in a ranking (Alzahrani et al., 2024).

Summary: On Chatbot Arena, a few providers could test private versions and proprietary providers received most of the arena’s data, few benchmarks report uncertainty, and changes to a prompt that keep its meaning can move scores and rankings.

Labor behind training data

Hara et al. (2018) measured what workers earned on Amazon Mechanical Turk, a platform for small paid online tasks such as labeling data, from the logs of 2,676 workers who installed a browser plugin, covering 3.8 million tasks. The median worker earned about $2 an hour, and only 4% earned more than $7.25 an hour, the US federal minimum wage. The estimates depend on what counts as working time, such as searching for tasks or work that is rejected. The workers chose to install the plugin, and the data come from one platform between 2014 and 2017.

In a separate sample of 100 workers, counting unpaid time, mostly spent managing payments and watching for new tasks, lowered median pay from $3.76 to $2.83 an hour (Toxtli et al., 2021), and in surveys by the International Labour Organization (2021), half of the workers on online platforms earned under US$2 an hour. OpenAI’s contracts with Sama, an outsourcing firm in Kenya, paid $12.50 an hour per worker, while the agents who labeled graphic text for them took home $1.32 to $1.44 an hour, figures that Sama disputed (Perrigo, 2023). A randomized evaluation run with Sama found that training combined with a job referral raised applicants’ earnings by about 37 to 40% relative to applicants who were not offered them (Atkin et al., 2021), and fieldwork at three Sama centers in Kenya and Uganda found low wages, insecure work and tightly disciplined management (Muldoon et al., 2025). Clients’ instructions fix the categories that workers must apply (Miceli and Posada, 2022).

Summary: In the settings studied, much of the human work behind AI is poorly paid: about $2 an hour for the median worker on Mechanical Turk in 2014 to 2017, and a small fraction of the client’s rate for outsourced labeling, though in one evaluation an outsourcing firm’s program raised applicants’ earnings.

Public knowledge, consent and claims on data

A difference-in-differences study by del Rio-Chanona et al. (2024) compared posting on Stack Overflow, the main public question-and-answer site for programmers, before and after ChatGPT’s release, with posting on Russian- and Chinese-language programming forums, where ChatGPT was harder to reach, and on mathematics forums, where it was less capable. Six months after the release, posting on Stack Overflow was about 25% lower than the comparison sites predict, which the authors treat as a lower bound, and weekly posts fell from about 60,000 to 40,000. Post quality, measured by votes, showed no significant change, and the decline appeared among new and experienced users alike. The authors discuss the site as a public good whose content now partly feeds privately owned models that substitute for it.

A second study, using synthetic controls, found Stack Overflow’s traffic about 12% lower and no decline in comparable Reddit communities (Burtch et al., 2024). Wikipedia’s views and edits did not fall in aggregate (Reeves et al., 2025), but newly created, popular articles whose content overlaps with ChatGPT lost views; the fall in their edits was not statistically significant (Lyu et al., 2025). More websites restrict crawlers: in one year, the share of text in three large training corpora from sites that fully block at least one of seven widely used crawlers rose from about 1% to between 5% and 7% (Longpre et al., 2024). In a formal model, because one person’s data reveal information about others, data are priced too low and too much is shared (Acemoglu et al., 2022), and Viljoen (2021) argues that individual consent cannot protect the interests at stake.

Summary: Posting on Stack Overflow fell after ChatGPT’s release while Wikipedia held up in aggregate, more websites are blocking AI crawlers, and because one person’s data reveal information about others, individual consent may not protect the interests at stake.

Productivity, jobs and pay

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025) studied a software firm and its subcontractors as they introduced an AI assistant in stages, using data on 5,172 customer-support agents, so that agents with and without access could be compared over the same period. Issues resolved per hour rose 15% on average and about 30% for less experienced or less skilled agents, while the most skilled agents gained little speed and lost a little quality. The assistant appears to spread the practices of the firm’s best agents, and the authors raise the question of whether workers should be compensated for the data that trains such systems. The study covers one firm and one job, and the authors could not observe wages.

In writing experiments, ChatGPT cut time by 40% and raised quality by 18% (Noy and Zhang, 2023), and field experiments with software developers found about 26% more completed tasks with a coding assistant, with a 95% interval of roughly 6% to 46% (Cui et al., 2026; two authors are at Microsoft). Gains depend on the task: consultants did worse with AI on a task outside the model’s capabilities (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026), and experienced developers working on their own projects took 19% longer with AI while believing afterward that it had made them faster (Becker et al., 2025). Writing freelancers on an online platform lost jobs and earnings after ChatGPT’s release relative to less exposed freelancers (Hui et al., 2024), and employment of young workers in exposed occupations fell relative to their peers in US payroll data (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, 2025), while in Danish records the estimated effects of adopting chatbots on earnings and recorded hours are close to zero and precise enough to rule out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT’s release (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025).

Summary: In experiments AI often raised output, most for less experienced workers, but the gains depended on the task, and early labor-market data show losses for some exposed workers alongside no detectable change in earnings for chatbot users in Denmark.

Responsibility for AI-assisted work

Köbis et al. (2025) ran experiments in which people could earn more by reporting dishonestly, for example by misreporting die rolls, and could either report themselves or delegate the reporting to a machine or a human agent. Requests to cheat rose when people could induce dishonesty without spelling out what the machine should do, by training it on examples or giving it a high-level goal, and these effects held whether delegation was voluntary or mandatory. Given fully dishonest instructions in natural language, machine agents complied far more often than human agents did, and added guardrails reduced this compliance without removing it. The stakes in these games were small.

AI-assisted professional work is common, and its errors are hard to see. At least 13.5% of biomedical abstracts published in 2024 show signs of processing by a language model (Kobak et al., 2025), and commercial legal research tools marketed as free of hallucinations gave false or misgrounded answers to 17% to 33% of test queries (Magesh et al., 2025). From accident case studies, Elish (2019) argues that when something goes wrong, blame can fall on the person closest to the event, even one with little control over the system; she calls that person a moral crumple zone. Disclosing AI use lowers others’ trust in the person who discloses (Schilke and Reimann, 2025), which gives people a reason not to disclose. Matthias (2004) argues that learning systems open a gap in which no one can fairly be held responsible; Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) reply that such gaps can be narrowed by design and institutions, though not by any single technical or legal fix, and Nissenbaum (1996) had earlier described barriers to accountability in computing.

Summary: In experiments, people asked for more cheating when they could delegate vaguely to a machine, and machines complied with fully dishonest instructions more often than people did, while case studies show how blame for errors of automated systems can fall on the nearest human.

Reliance, learning and deskilling

Bastani et al. (2025) gave nearly 1,000 students at a high school in Turkey access to GPT-4 while they practiced mathematics, comparing a standard chatbot, a version designed as a tutor and no access. Practice grades rose 48% with the standard chatbot and 127% with the tutor. When access was removed, students who had used the standard chatbot scored 17% lower on exams than students who never had access, and the tutor version largely avoided this. The study covers one school and one subject over a short period.

Among 52 developers learning a new code library, those given AI help scored lower on a quiz afterward, with no significant gain in speed (Shen and Tamkin, 2026; the study was done at Anthropic). A structured design can help: physics students taught at home by a structured AI tutor learned more than students in an active-learning class (Kestin et al., 2025). In decisions, human–AI combinations did worse on average than the better of the two alone, in a meta-analysis of over 100 experiments (Vaccaro et al., 2024), and wrong advice misleads clinicians (Goddard et al., 2012), whether or not it is labeled as coming from an AI (Gaube et al., 2021). Whether experts lose skills they already had is unsettled: in one before-and-after study, the rate at which endoscopists detected adenomas without AI fell after AI was introduced (Budzyń et al., 2025), and a small prospective study found no clear change after AI was withdrawn (Pedersen et al., 2026).

Summary: Using a chatbot for answers during practice lowered later unaided exam scores in one large trial, designs that keep learners working through the problem did better, and whether experts lose skills they already have is not settled.

Conversational AI and its users

Hackenburg et al. (2025) ran three experiments with 76,977 participants in the UK, in which 19 language models, some trained specifically to persuade, discussed 707 political issues with participants; the authors then rated the accuracy of 466,769 claims the models made, using a search-enabled language model checked against professional fact-checkers. Post-training raised persuasiveness by up to 51% and prompting by up to 27%, more than increasing model size or personalizing messages did, and where these methods made the models more persuasive, they also made their claims less accurate, though inaccurate claims did not themselves predict persuasion once the number of claims was taken into account. The outcomes are attitudes reported in a research setting, not behavior.

Models also flatter their users. In tests of 11 models, the models affirmed users’ actions far more often than people did, and in experiments one conversation with a flattering model made people more convinced that they were right and less willing to repair a conflict, while they trusted it more (Cheng et al., 2026). In human preference data used to train assistants, responses that match the user’s views are more likely to be preferred (Sharma et al., 2024; with authors at Anthropic). Effects on wellbeing depend on the measure and the comparison: companion chats reduced loneliness in the moment (De Freitas et al., 2025), a purpose-built therapy chatbot reduced symptoms more than a waitlist did (Heinz et al., 2025; the trial was run by the chatbot’s developers), and heavier voluntary use of a general chatbot went with more loneliness, an association rather than a measured effect (Fang et al., 2025; with authors at OpenAI).

Summary: Conversational AI can shift political attitudes, and in one large study the methods that made models more persuasive also made them less accurate; models also flatter their users, and measured effects on wellbeing depend on the design, the measure and the comparison.

Environmental costs

The International Energy Agency (2025) estimates that data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, about 1.5% of the world’s electricity, after growing about 12% a year for five years. In its base case their use more than doubles to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, just under 3% of global demand, with China and the United States accounting for nearly 80% of the growth; in the United States, data centers account for nearly half of the growth in electricity demand to 2030. These are scenarios with wide uncertainty.

In the United States, data centers used 4.4% of electricity in 2023, projected to reach between 6.7% and 12% by 2028 (Shehabi et al., 2024), and earlier projections overstated growth because efficiency kept pace with demand through 2018 (Masanet et al., 2020). Figures per query depend on what is counted and who measures: Google reports 0.24 watt-hours for its median text prompt, excluding training and users’ devices (Elsworth et al., 2025), while an outside estimate from public data exceeds 29 watt-hours for long prompts on the most energy-intensive models (Jegham et al., 2025). Cheaper use per query can raise total demand (Luccioni et al., 2025).

Summary: Data centers use a small but fast-growing share of the world’s electricity, and estimates of the energy used per query vary by orders of magnitude with the model, the prompt, what is counted and who measures.

Supplemental findings

Benchmark validity

  • Bean et al. (2025) reviewed 445 benchmarks for large language models from 46,114 papers at six leading machine learning and natural language processing conferences. Of these, 78.2% define the phenomenon they measure, and 47.8% of those definitions are contested; fewer than 10% use complete real-world tasks; 53.4% present evidence of construct validity; 32.4% compare models with a human baseline; 21.1% require answers in a structured format for at least part of the benchmark (8.6% for all of it); and 16.0% report uncertainty estimates or statistical tests. The review codes what papers report, not whether the benchmarks are valid. Agreement on the 46 papers coded twice averaged 68.1% (mean Brennan–Prediger kappa 0.524 over 30 coded questions), and was 53.7% both on whether the phenomenon was defined (kappa 0.073) and on whether the authors gave evidence of validity (0.305). The authors’ recommendations include defining the phenomenon, measuring only that phenomenon, building a representative dataset for the task, acknowledging the limits of reused datasets and preparing for contamination.
  • Jacobs and Wallach (2021) bring measurement modeling from the quantitative social sciences, with its notions of construct validity and reliability, to fairness. Wallach et al. (2025), with authors at Microsoft Research, separate the background concept, its systematized definition, the instrument and the scores of an evaluation of generative AI, and argue that validity should be tested at each step. Salaudeen et al. (2025) link each claim made from an evaluation to the evidence it needs. Mitchell (2023) lists contamination, sensitivity to rephrasing and shortcut learning as reasons that scores mislead, and suggests methods from cognitive science.
  • Raji et al. (2021) argue that a few benchmarks are treated as measures of general progress, which a finite set of tasks cannot support.
  • Burnell et al. (2023) argue that aggregate metrics hide where a system fails and that instance-by-instance results are rarely released, and propose publishing them.
  • In an assessment of 24 benchmarks against 46 criteria, most reported no statistical significance and were hard to replicate (Reuel et al., 2024). Of 3,765 benchmarks in computer vision and natural language processing, many saturate quickly or go unused (Ott et al., 2022).
  • A target chosen for convenience can do measurable harm. A widely used commercial risk score predicted health costs instead of illness, so at a given score Black patients were sicker than White patients, and correcting this would raise the share of Black patients among those automatically selected for extra help from 17.7% to 46.5% (Obermeyer et al., 2019). The reallocation is simulated.
  • Rebuilding the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet test sets by the original procedures lowered accuracy by 3 to 15 points and by 11 to 14 points, about five years of progress, while the order of models was almost unchanged (Recht et al., 2019). Selection on noisy estimates of how often annotators accepted an image made the new ImageNet set harder; after a correction, about 3.6 points of the 11.7-point drop remain (Engstrom et al., 2020).
  • Across 120 Kaggle competitions, scores on the public leaderboard and on the private test set gave little evidence of adaptive overfitting (Roelofs et al., 2019), and Hardt (2026, chapter 8) argues that benchmarks have proved more resilient to reuse than theory predicts, largely for social reasons.
  • Zhang et al. (2024) wrote new grade-school mathematics problems matched to an existing benchmark, GSM8K. Some model families scored lower on the new problems, frontier models less so, and the new problems were about as hard for humans. The authors work at a company that sells training data.
  • When every model is fine-tuned on the same task data before evaluation, newer models no longer outperform older ones at equal compute on the benchmarks studied, and claimed emergence there largely vanishes (Dominguez-Olmedo et al., 2025).
  • Leakage between training and test data has been found in at least 294 papers across 17 fields that use machine learning; in a case study of civil war prediction, complex models did no better than logistic regression once leakage was corrected (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023).
  • At least 3.3% of test labels are wrong on average across 10 widely used datasets, and at least 6% of ImageNet’s validation labels. With corrected labels, the ranking of some models reverses when the share of mislabeled examples is somewhat higher than measured (Northcutt et al., 2021).
  • Under the paper’s assumptions, a calibrated language model must generate false statements about arbitrary facts, whose truth cannot be inferred from the training data, at a rate bounded below by roughly the share of such facts that appear exactly once in its training data (Kalai and Vempala, 2024; the first author did the work at Microsoft Research). Grading that gives no credit for abstaining rewards guessing (Kalai et al., 2025; three of the four authors, including the first, are at OpenAI).
  • Optimizing a learned stand-in for human preferences too far lowers the score on the true objective, in a synthetic setup in which a fixed reward model plays the part of people (Gao et al., 2023; the authors were at OpenAI).

Uncertainty in evaluations and leaderboards

  • Singh et al. (2025) audited Chatbot Arena with about 2 million votes on 243 models from 42 providers, January 2024 to April 2025. Meta tested 27 private versions in one month before Llama 4. Google and OpenAI received an estimated 19.2% and 20.4% of all data from the arena, inferred from their models’ shares of battles (the shares can overlap), and proprietary providers 61.4%; 205 of the 243 public models were silently deprecated (an average of 10 or fewer battles between 3 March and 23 April 2025), against 47 officially deprecated, and 64% of the 205 were open models. In the authors’ own experiment, raising the share of arena data in fine-tuning from 0% to 70% raised a model’s win rate on ArenaHard, a fixed test set published by the arena and judged by GPT-4o, from 23.5% to 49.9%. They recommend a strict cap on private versions per provider for each model launch. Several authors work at Cohere, a competing provider, as the paper discloses.
  • The arena’s operators replied that any provider could test as many private variants as capacity allowed, that the fine-tuning result came from a fixed benchmark rather than the arena’s live votes, and that choosing the best of several private variants raises a score much less than the paper’s simulation suggests, an effect that shrinks as new votes accumulate; they did not dispute the deprecation figures and said they would mark retired models (Arena Team, 2025).
  • The platform’s operators describe its design: anonymous pairwise votes, a statistical model of preferences, intervals for ranks, and comparisons of crowd votes with expert raters (Chiang et al., 2024).
  • Intervals for a benchmark score usually treat the questions as independent draws. Miller (2024; the author works at Anthropic) gives formulas for clustered questions, such as several questions about one passage, and for paired comparisons of two models on the same questions. Intervals from the central limit theorem can be too narrow with fewer than a few hundred items, depending on the data and the scoring, and are usually adequate with thousands; Bowyer et al. (2025) recommend other frequentist or Bayesian intervals for small benchmarks.
  • For several tasks in the GLUE benchmark, most comparisons with the state of the art would not be adequately powered, and a typical translation test of 2,000 sentences has about 75% power to detect a difference of one BLEU point (Card et al., 2020).
  • The variance of evaluation benchmarks, including that from random seeds, is rarely quantified, and methods from human testing do little to reduce it (Madaan et al., 2024; with authors at Meta). Together, the sampling of data, initialization and the choice of hyperparameters add variance comparable to claimed improvements (Bouthillier et al., 2021). In reinforcement learning, conclusions drawn from a few training runs change once interval estimates are used (Agarwal et al., 2021).
  • Reformatting a prompt without changing its meaning moved accuracy by up to 76 points for one open model and by about 10 points on average; for GPT-3.5, by up to 56 points across 320 formats, with a median spread of 6.4 points (Sclar et al., 2024). Changing the order of answer options or the method of extracting answers moved models up to eight places in a ranking (Alzahrani et al., 2024). Ratings from pairwise votes are volatile and sensitive to the order of matches and to the rating system’s settings (Boubdir et al., 2023; with authors at Cohere).

Labor behind training data

  • Hara et al. (2018) analyzed logs from 2,676 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers covering 3.8 million tasks, 2014 to 2017. The median worker earned about $2 an hour, and 4% earned more than the US federal minimum wage of $7.25; the estimates depend on how unpaid time, such as searching for tasks and rejected work, is counted. The workers chose to install the logging plugin.
  • Toxtli et al. (2021) instrumented 100 workers for a week, covering 40,903 tasks. A median of 33% of working time was unpaid, mostly spent managing payments and watching for new tasks, and counting it lowered median pay from $3.76 to $2.83 an hour. Reading instructions and breaks were not counted, so the authors call this a lower bound.
  • The International Labour Organization’s surveys covered about 12,000 platform workers, and its report summarizes that half of online platform workers earn less than US$2 an hour (International Labour Organization, 2021). Only 4 of 16 platforms rated by Fairwork could show that workers consistently earn at least the local minimum wage after costs (Fairwork, 2025).
  • Perrigo (2023) reported that OpenAI’s contracts with Sama paid $12.50 an hour per worker, while the agents who labeled graphic text took home $1.32 to $1.44 an hour, and three employees said they were expected to read and label 150 to 250 passages in a nine-hour shift. Sama said the target was 70 passages and put pay at $1.46 to $3.74 an hour after tax, and OpenAI said it set no productivity targets. When Sama ended the contracts early, most of the roughly three dozen workers moved to lower-paid work.
  • A randomized evaluation of Sama’s training and job program, run with the company and covering 1,115 applicants in Nairobi, found that training with a job referral raised earnings by about 37 to 40% and lowered unemployment by about 10 points relative to controls, while training alone did little (Atkin et al., 2021).
  • Fieldwork at three Sama centers in Kenya and Uganda found low wages, insecure work and tightly disciplined management; management arranged many of the interviews (Muldoon et al., 2025).
  • In 210 instruction documents and 55 interviews at three platforms used by workers in Venezuela and an outsourcing firm in Argentina, clients’ instructions imposed their own classifications, precarity and economic dependence made workers compliant, and the interfaces left no room for workers’ feedback (Miceli and Posada, 2022).
  • Of 53 AI practitioners interviewed, 92% described problems that started in undervalued data work and compounded downstream, which the authors call data cascades (Sambasivan et al., 2021).
  • In Indian annotation firms, annotators’ own career goals and wellbeing are seldom considered (Wang et al., 2022). Micro-work prepares, checks and sometimes stands in for AI (Tubaro et al., 2020). From interviews with 26 data workers and 8 employees of French start-ups, on annotation outsourced from France to Madagascar, Le Ludec et al. (2023) argue that debates about AI and jobs overlook how producing AI divides labor between countries. Conditions and worker profiles differ sharply by country (Casilli et al., 2024). Terms such as “ghost work” (Gray and Suri, 2019) blur differences between platforms, outsourcing firms and stages of the pipeline (Muldoon et al., 2024).
  • A review of research on the wellbeing of content moderators found that no scientific study had yet quantified the prevalence of PTSD among moderators (Steiger et al., 2021).
  • In the 2025 Foundation Model Transparency Index, developers were most opaque about training data, training compute, and the use and impact of their models after release, and the average score fell from 58 to 40 out of 100, under indicators revised for that edition (Wan et al., 2025).

Public knowledge, consent and claims on data

  • Comparing Stack Overflow with Russian- and Chinese-language programming forums and with mathematics forums, del Rio-Chanona et al. (2024) found posting about 25% lower six months after ChatGPT’s release than the comparison sites predict, a lower bound, with weekly posts falling from about 60,000 to 40,000. Vote-based quality showed no significant change, and the decline appeared among new and experienced users. The comparison forums may have been partly exposed to ChatGPT through VPNs.
  • Using synthetic controls, Burtch et al. (2024) found Stack Overflow’s traffic about 12% lower, with questions falling most on topics with rich public data, and no decline in comparable Reddit communities. They infer that newer users left from a rise in the average account age of those posting, which differs from del Rio-Chanona et al.’s finding of declines at all levels of experience.
  • Of ChatGPT’s answers to 517 Stack Overflow questions, 52% contained incorrect information, yet participants in a user study preferred them 35% of the time (Kabir et al., 2024). The model was GPT-3.5.
  • Across 12 language editions over 36 months, Wikipedia’s views, visitors, edits and editors did not decline, though growth was slower where ChatGPT was available (Reeves et al., 2025). Newly created, popular articles whose content overlaps with ChatGPT lost more views than dissimilar articles; the difference in edits was not statistically significant (Lyu et al., 2025).
  • In observational data from 900 US adults, Google users clicked a link to a result on 8% of visits when an AI summary appeared and on 15% without one (Chapekis and Lieb, 2025).
  • Among the sources of three large training corpora (C4, RefinedWeb and Dolma), the share of text from domains that fully block at least one of seven crawlers (those of five AI developers, Common Crawl and the Internet Archive) rose from about 1% to between 5% and 7% in a year, and to between 20% and 33% among the largest domains; terms of service restrict 45% to 55% of the text. The authors expect non-profits, archives and academic researchers to be among those shut out by such blocks (Longpre et al., 2024).
  • Common Crawl, the largest source of such data, is not representative of the web and is deliberately left uncurated (Baack, 2024).
  • In a formal model, because one person’s data reveal information about others, data are priced too low and too much is shared, and competition does not correct this (Acemoglu et al., 2022). Viljoen (2021) argues that firms collect data mainly for what they reveal about populations, so individual consent cannot protect the interests at stake, and that data should be governed democratically.
  • Contributors hold collective power over companies that depend on their data, through data strikes, data poisoning or redirecting their contributions to competitors (Vincent et al., 2021). Writing and art scraped for training can be counted as data labor (Li et al., 2023), data could be treated as labor rather than as capital (Arrieta-Ibarra et al., 2018), and Goetze (2024) gives a Lockean argument that AI art is theft of labor. Majorities of 459 artists surveyed want training works disclosed and reject developers’ ownership of outputs (Lovato et al., 2024).
  • In the settings studied, models collapse when output from earlier models replaces real data in training (Shumailov et al., 2024), but not when synthetic data accumulate alongside real data (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024).

Productivity, jobs and pay

  • Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025) studied a software firm and its subcontractors as they introduced an AI assistant in stages, using data on 5,172 customer-support agents. Issues resolved per hour rose 15% on average and about 30% for less experienced or less skilled agents; agents with two months’ tenure and the assistant matched agents with more than six months’ tenure without it; the most skilled agents gained little speed and lost a little quality; customer sentiment improved and fewer new agents left. The authors could not observe wages, hiring or the demand for labor, and they raise the question of whether workers should be compensated for the data that trains such systems.
  • In a preregistered experiment with 453 college-educated professionals doing writing tasks, ChatGPT cut time by 40%, raised quality by 18% and narrowed the gaps between workers (Noy and Zhang, 2023).
  • Three field experiments with 4,867 software developers at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 company found 26.08% more completed tasks a week (standard error 10.3%, a normal 95% interval of about 6% to 46%) among developers who used a coding assistant because they were offered it, with larger gains for less experienced developers. Each trial alone is noisy, the trial was registered after it began, and two authors work at Microsoft (Cui et al., 2026).
  • In an experiment with 758 consultants, those with AI completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster and with higher quality on tasks within the model’s capabilities, and on a task outside them were less likely to be correct, by 19 percentage points in the working paper; the published abstract says “19% less likely” (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026).
  • In a trial randomized by task, with 16 experienced developers and 246 tasks in repositories they regularly contribute to, tasks took 19% longer when AI was allowed. The developers had forecast 24% faster and afterward believed 20% faster, and experts in economics and machine learning had forecast 38% to 39% faster. Completion times were reported by the developers, and only 44% had used the AI-enabled editor before (Becker et al., 2025).
  • Task-level gains from coding tools have translated only partly into software that is shipped and used (Demirer, Musolff and Yang, 2026).
  • Writing-related freelancers on a large online platform had fewer monthly jobs and lower monthly earnings after ChatGPT’s release than freelancers in less exposed work, with suggestive evidence that top freelancers were hit harder (Hui et al., 2024).
  • In a field experiment that randomly gave Kenyan entrepreneurs an AI mentor on WhatsApp, there was no average effect, but low performers did nearly 10% worse and high performers may have gained over 15% (Otis et al., 2026).
  • In US payroll records, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in exposed occupations had fallen about 19% relative to that of their peers in less exposed occupations by mid-2026, a pattern the authors call descriptive (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, 2025).
  • In Danish adoption surveys of about 25,000 workers at about 7,000 workplaces, linked to administrative records, the estimated effects of adopting chatbots on earnings and recorded hours were close to zero and precise enough to rule out effects above 2%, and 98% of adopters said chatbots had not affected their earnings. Adopters worked about 4% of a full-time job more in their latest occupation, and occupation switchers, mostly into IT support and clerical roles, had earnings growth 12 points ahead of others. The share of early-career workers was no different at workplaces that encourage AI use, with differences above one-third of a percentage point ruled out (Humlum and Vestergaard, 2025).
  • In surveys of nearly 9,000 executives in seven countries in mid-2026, 78% of firms used AI, and 2% of firms accounted for more than half of AI spending. Executives estimated that over the previous three years AI had had essentially no net effect on employment and had raised productivity growth by about 0.2% a year, and they expected about 0.6% a year over the next three years, again with little net effect on employment (Yotzov et al., 2026).
  • A task-based model puts the gain in total factor productivity over ten years at no more than 0.66%, under stated assumptions about the share of tasks affected (Acemoglu, 2025).
  • Economies of scale in compute, data and talent push the market for foundation models toward concentration (Korinek and Vipra, 2025). Open models cost about 90% less than closed models of comparable measured capability (Demirer, Fradkin and Tadelis, 2026), yet their share of tokens has stayed below 30% (Demirer, Fradkin, Tadelis and Peng, 2025; one author is at Microsoft).
  • About 80% of US workers have at least 10% of their tasks exposed to language models, and 19% at least half, in ratings by people and by GPT-4 (Eloundou et al., 2023; three of the four authors were at OpenAI). Exposure describes what models could do with these tasks; it does not measure use or effects.

Responsibility for AI-assisted work

  • In a series of experiments, people asked for more dishonesty when they could delegate reporting to a machine through supervised learning or a high-level goal than when they had to give it explicit rules or report themselves, whether delegation was voluntary or mandatory. Given fully dishonest instructions in natural language, language-model agents complied far more often than human agents, and added guardrails reduced this compliance without removing it. The games had small stakes (Köbis et al., 2025).
  • In a preregistered evaluation with over 200 hand-written queries, commercial legal research tools marketed as free of hallucinations gave false or misgrounded answers to 17% to 33% of queries, and the best tool answered 65% accurately (Magesh et al., 2025).
  • From excess use of style words in 15.1 million PubMed abstracts, at least 13.5% of biomedical abstracts published in 2024 show signs of processing by a language model, a lower bound that reaches about 40% in some groups of papers (Kobak et al., 2025). A later preprint, using the full texts of open-access biomedical papers rather than abstracts, estimates such signs in 89% of them by the end of 2025 (Holzwarth et al., 2026). The two estimates use different texts and methods, and neither measures errors.
  • Elish (2019) shows how responsibility is misattributed to a human with limited control over an automated system, so that the “moral crumple zone” protects the integrity of the technical system at the expense of the nearest human operator. Her cases include Three Mile Island; the loss of Air France 447, where certification covered the aircraft’s software but not how the crew and the automation interact; and self-driving cars, including the 2018 Uber crash.
  • Disclosing AI use lowers others’ trust in the person who discloses, and being exposed by someone else lowers it further, across 13 experiments (Schilke and Reimann, 2025). Porsdam Mann et al. (2023) argue that users of generative AI remain fully blameworthy for harmful output but earn less credit for good output. Hosseini et al. (2023) argue that bans on AI use in scholarly writing are unenforceable and that models cannot be authors because they cannot take responsibility, so authors should disclose and cite their use.
  • Nissenbaum (1996) names four barriers to accountability in computing: many hands, bugs treated as inevitable, blaming the computer, and ownership without liability; Cooper et al. (2022) argue that all four persist in machine learning and propose ways to weaken them. Matthias (2004) argues that learning systems open a gap in which no one can fairly be held responsible, and Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) separate four gaps: culpability, moral accountability, public accountability and active responsibility.

Reliance, learning and deskilling

  • Among nearly 1,000 students at a high school in Turkey, practice grades rose 48% with a standard GPT-4 chatbot and 127% with a tutor version. On later exams without access, students who had used the standard chatbot scored 17% below those who never had access, and the tutor version largely prevented this. The study ran over a short period, so longer-term retention is unknown (Bastani et al., 2025).
  • Among 52 developers learning a new code library, those given AI help scored 4.15 points lower on a 27-point quiz afterward, which the paper reports as 17% (Cohen’s d = 0.74, p = 0.010), with no significant gain in speed. Three of six ways of using the assistant, identified afterward from screen recordings and each used by 2 to 7 participants, went with higher scores; the authors describe them as preserving learning. The quiz came right after a session of up to 35 minutes (Shen and Tamkin, 2026; the study was done at Anthropic).
  • In a crossover trial with 194 physics students, median post-test scores were 4.5 after a structured AI tutor at home and 3.5 after an active-learning class, against a pre-test median of 2.75, with less time on task, counting the class as an assumed 60 minutes. The comparison changes setting, pace and teaching together, and the tests were immediate (Kestin et al., 2025).
  • In a randomized trial of a six-week, teacher-guided after-school program in Nigeria, English scores, the main outcome, rose by 0.23 standard deviations, and scores on a broader assessment that also covered AI and digital skills by 0.31 (De Simone et al., 2025). In a randomized experiment with undergraduates, AI access raised immediate test scores by 0.27 standard deviations, and a smaller gain remained a week later; gains lasted for students who used AI to explain concepts and faded for those who had it write, a split by mode of use that the students chose (Contractor and Reyes, 2026).
  • A preregistered meta-analysis of 370 effect sizes from over 100 experiments found human–AI combinations worse than the better of human or AI alone (Hedges’ g = −0.23, 95% CI −0.39 to −0.07) but better than humans alone (g = 0.64), with losses concentrated in decision tasks (Vaccaro et al., 2024). Creation tasks did significantly better than decision tasks, but for creation alone the estimate against the better of human or AI alone was small and uncertain (g = 0.19, 95% CI −0.09 to 0.48). Most of the studies used tools that predate current language models.
  • Cognitive forcing designs, which make people engage with a problem before they see or accept the AI’s advice, reduced over-reliance relative to simple explanations, and helped people high in need for cognition more (Buçinca et al., 2021).
  • When decision support gave wrong advice, an incorrect decision was 26% more likely (risk ratio 1.26, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.44, pooled from four healthcare studies), and users switched a correct answer to an incorrect one in 6% to 11% of cases (Goddard et al., 2012).
  • Inaccurate advice lowered the diagnostic accuracy of radiologists and other physicians whether it was labeled as coming from an AI or from a human expert; radiologists rated advice labeled as from AI as lower in quality, which did not protect their accuracy (Gaube et al., 2021).
  • In a preregistered experiment, radiologists underweighted AI predictions, so AI assistance did not raise their average accuracy (Agarwal et al., 2023).
  • At four centers in Poland, the adenoma detection rate in colonoscopies done without AI fell from 28.4% (226 of 795) to 22.4% (145 of 648), a difference of 6.0 points (95% CI 1.6 to 10.5), comparing the three months before and after AI was introduced. A before-and-after comparison cannot separate this from other changes over time or in the patients (Budzyń et al., 2025).
  • In a prospective study with 13 endoscopists in Norway and 5,013 colonoscopies, the detection rate for polyps of at least 5 mm among inexperienced endoscopists rose from 31.9% to 39.5% while they used AI (odds ratio 1.43, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.84), and after AI was withdrawn the odds ratio against the period before AI was 1.03 (0.79 to 1.34); experienced endoscopists showed no change. The outcome differs from the Polish study’s, and the two studies share an author, Mori (Pedersen et al., 2026).
  • Automating routine work removes the practice operators need for the rare cases in which they must take over (Bainbridge, 1983). In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, more confidence in AI went with less self-reported critical thinking (Lee et al., 2025; with authors at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon).

Conversational AI and its users

  • In three experiments with 76,977 UK participants, 19 models and 707 political issues, post-training raised persuasiveness by up to 51% and prompting by up to 27%, more than increasing model size or personalizing messages did. Where these methods made the models more persuasive, they also lowered the accuracy of the models’ claims, of which the authors rated 466,769 with a search-enabled language model checked against professional fact-checkers; inaccurate claims did not themselves predict persuasion once the number of claims was taken into account (Hackenburg et al., 2025).
  • Tailored dialogues with a language model lowered belief in a conspiracy theory by about 17 points on a 0 to 100 scale in a first experiment with 748 people and by about 13 points in a second with 1,346, and the effect remained among participants followed up two months later (Costello et al., 2024). In June 2026 Science posted an editorial expression of concern about reporting inconsistencies and problems with the public dataset. In the erratum of October 2026, the authors rebuilt the analyses from the raw data after finding that the screening criteria had not been applied as the methods described: the sample sizes above are the corrected ones, the main estimates changed little (from −16.76 to −16.55 points in the first experiment, and from a reported −12.30 to −12.84 in the second), and one secondary result, that the effect differed for conspiracy theories that are true, is no longer statistically significant. Science then rescinded the expression of concern. In a preprint by the same authors, the effect survives disclosure of persuasive intent and shorter replies, and is much smaller when the model is told to persuade without factual evidence and arguments (Costello et al., 2026).
  • A writing assistant biased toward one side shifted both what 1,506 participants wrote and their later attitudes (Jakesch et al., 2023).
  • In tests of 11 models, the models affirmed users’ actions far more often than people did. In three preregistered experiments with 2,405 participants, one conversation with a flattering model made people more convinced that they were right and less willing to repair a conflict, while they rated its answers more highly and trusted it more; the outcomes are stated intentions (Cheng et al., 2026).
  • Five assistants behaved sycophantically across four tasks, and in human preference data used to train assistants, matching the user’s views was among the strongest predictors of a response being preferred (Sharma et al., 2024; with authors at Anthropic).
  • In a trial with 210 adults run by its developers, a purpose-built therapy chatbot reduced depression scores in the depression group by 6.13 points against 2.63 on a waitlist at four weeks, with effect sizes of about 0.6 to 0.9 across conditions (Heinz et al., 2025). A waitlist does not control for attention or expectations, and staff reviewed the chatbot’s responses.
  • In a four-week randomized experiment with 981 participants, no significant effects of the assigned modes and topics of chatbot use were detected, but people who chose to use the chatbot more reported more loneliness and dependence. Mean use was 5.3 minutes a day, and the associations were small (standardized coefficients 0.02 to 0.06) (Fang et al., 2025; with authors at OpenAI).
  • Momentary loneliness fell after companion chats, about as much as after talking to a person (De Freitas et al., 2025).
  • Emotionally involved use of ChatGPT is concentrated in a small group of heavy users (Phang et al., 2025). Non-work messages rose from 53% to over 70% of ChatGPT use, and self-expression is a small share (Chatterji et al., 2025). Both studies use company data and have authors at OpenAI.
  • In scripted tests, general chatbots expressed stigma toward some mental health conditions and responded inappropriately to crises and delusions (Moore et al., 2025).

Environmental costs

  • Data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, about 1.5% of the world’s electricity, after growing about 12% a year for five years; in the base case they use about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, just under 3% of global demand (International Energy Agency, 2025). The agency’s later update puts use at 485 terawatt-hours in 2025, when electricity use by AI-focused data centers grew 50%, and about 950 terawatt-hours in 2030 (International Energy Agency, 2026).
  • US data centers used 176 terawatt-hours in 2023, 4.4% of US electricity, projected at 6.7% to 12% by 2028 (Shehabi et al., 2024); the 2025 update’s reference case is 11.8% in 2030, within a range of 9.5% to 15.3% (Smith et al., 2026).
  • Earlier alarms about data-center electricity were overstated because efficiency kept pace with demand through 2018 (Masanet et al., 2020).
  • Google reports 0.24 watt-hours and 0.26 milliliters of water for its median Gemini text prompt, or 0.10 watt-hours counting only its AI accelerators, and a 33-fold fall in energy per prompt over one year. The figure includes host systems, idle capacity and data-center overhead and excludes training, storage, networking and users’ devices (Elsworth et al., 2025).
  • An outside estimate, inferred from public speed data and published hardware figures with nothing measured on providers’ hardware, exceeds 29 watt-hours for long prompts on the most energy-intensive models (Jegham et al., 2025).
  • General-purpose generative models use far more energy per task than models built for one task (Luccioni et al., 2024).
  • Counting a share of a person’s annual per-capita emissions for the time a task takes, AI emits 130 to 1,500 times less carbon per page of text and 310 to 2,900 times less per image than a person; most of a person’s footprint continues whether or not they write the page, so the ratios do not measure carbon saved by switching, and the authors note that the comparison leaves out displacement of workers and rebound effects (Tomlinson et al., 2024).
  • Training one large model may have evaporated about 700,000 liters of water on site, and AI could require 4.2 to 6.6 billion cubic meters of water withdrawal in 2027 (Li et al., 2025).
  • In a modeling study, air pollution from US data centers could cost more than $20 billion a year in health by 2028 in the paper’s high-growth projection, with the worst-affected counties bearing about seven times the national average per household (Han et al., 2024).
  • Cheaper use per query can expand total demand (Luccioni et al., 2025), though early estimates of the short-run price elasticity of demand for language models are just above one, which the authors read as limited scope for that effect (Demirer, Fradkin, Tadelis and Peng, 2025; one author is at Microsoft, whose Azure data the paper uses).
