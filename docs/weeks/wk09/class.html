Ethics of AI

Class

Author

Joshua Loftus

Note

This is the student version of the class sheet. The exercises are for your own time; the class works through everything under “In class”. Answers are revealed in class.

How this sheet works

Exercises rederive or apply the lecture’s results. Do them on your own time; they are not worked in class.

The class starts open. Discussion 1 is about what you are interested in or worried about. Discussion 2 turns to what could be done: each group is an ethics committee writing guidelines for the use of AI in one setting, using ideas from the course. The items under “If there is time” are more structured, and a class may take one of them up.

A guideline decides who checks, who is told, and who pays when something goes wrong. So each item ends by asking who bears the cost, who gains, or who the choice could be justified to.

Notation as in the lecture. A benchmark score is \(\theta + B + \bar\varepsilon\): \(\theta\) is what the benchmark is after, \(B\) is the effect of the prompt, judge and settings of one run, with variance \(\tau^2\), and \(\bar\varepsilon\) is the average noise over \(n\) items, with variance \(\sigma^2 / n\). \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function, and \(m_K\) is the expected largest of \(K\) independent standard normals.

Exercises

Attempt each part before you open a hint.

WarningHints

Do not open a hint unless you have been stuck for at least a few minutes, and open them in order.

Exercise 1. Fixed or random

A benchmark has \(n\) items, and a model answers about 80% of them correctly, so \(\sigma^2 = 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16\). Each run uses one prompt and one judge, and the choice of these moves the score with standard deviation \(\tau = 0.03\), that is, 3 points.

  1. With \(n = 100\), find the naive standard error, the true standard error when the target is \(\theta\), and the probability that the naive 95% interval contains \(\theta\).

  2. Repeat for \(n = 400\).

  3. A lab runs the 400 items under \(J\) different, equally reasonable setups and averages the \(J\) scores. How large must \(J\) be for the standard error of the average to be at most 2 points?

  4. A lab can publish the interval from (b) or the one from (c). Who gains from the narrower one being published, and who is misled by it?

TipHint 1

For (a): the naive standard error is \(\sigma / \sqrt n\), the true one \(\sqrt{\sigma^2 / n + \tau^2}\), and the coverage is \(2\,\Phi(1.96\, r) - 1\) with \(r\) their ratio.

TipHint 2

For (c): each setup draws its own \(B\), so the average of \(J\) runs has variance \((\sigma^2 / n + \tau^2) / J\).

Exercise 2. The best of \(K\)

On a leaderboard, the score of one version of a model has noise with standard deviation \(\sigma = 8\) Elo. Use \(m_{10} = 1.54\).

  1. A lab tests 10 private versions that are in truth equally good and publishes the best. By how much does the published score overstate, on average?

  2. Lab A tests 27 equally good versions and publishes the best. Lab B tests one. The two labs’ models are in truth equally good, with the same noise. Find the probability that A’s published score is above B’s.

  3. After publication, three fresh votes arrive for each vote from the testing period. Treating the score as an average over votes, how much of the overstatement in (a) remains?

  4. Who pays for the rank in (b)?

TipHint 1

For (b): there are 28 scores with the same distribution. Which one is the largest?

Exercise 3. New items

Invented numbers. A model answers 86% of the 1,200 problems in a public benchmark correctly. Researchers write 1,200 new problems by the same recipe, and people find them as hard as the old ones. The model answers 78% of the new problems correctly.

  1. Find the difference, its standard error and a 95% interval. Could chance explain the difference?

  2. Give two explanations other than chance. Which does the fact that people find the new problems as hard as the old ones support?

  3. In the lecture’s graph of the paths into a score, which arrow does the difference estimate?

  4. Who gains when 86% is the number reported?

TipHint 1

For (a): the two sets of problems are separate samples, so the variances of the two proportions add.

Exercise 4. Two averages

Requesters post paid tasks on a platform, and workers do them. Invented numbers: nine requesters pay $12 an hour and post 1 hour of work each; one requester pays $1 an hour and posts 91 hours.

  1. Find the average hourly rate over requesters, and the average over hours of work.

  2. A study of a real platform found that the average requester paid more than $11 an hour, and that the typical worker earned about $2 an hour (Hara et al., 2018). How can both be right?

  3. Which average would the platform quote, and whose experience does each one describe?

In class

Discussion 1: open floor

What about AI are you most interested in, or most worried about?

  1. First, think by yourself; and why do you think that? (~1 min)
  2. Discuss in small groups (~5 min)
  3. Share with class, find themes (~5-10 min)
  4. Class: who benefits, who might be harmed, and who can change this? (~2 min)

Discussion 2: an ethics committee

First, for two or three of the themes: what would have to be true for this worry to be smaller? Who could make it true?

Then each group is the ethics committee of one of these, a different one from every other group:

  • a university, for teaching and assessment;
  • an employer, such as a bank or a consultancy;
  • a government department that decides on benefits or visas;
  • a hospital;
  • a newsroom.

The committee has been asked for guidelines on the use of AI, and to design the conditions around its use. Write at most five points. Be specific.

Then swap with another committee. Read their guidelines as someone the guidelines would hurt, or as someone who wants to get around them. Which point fails first, and who does the failure fall on?

If there is time

Discussion 3: deskilling

Deskilling: work redesigned so that it needs less skill, or a person’s skill fading because a tool does the task.

  1. Think of a skill that a tool now does for most people: mental arithmetic, spelling, finding your way, writing code. Has anything been lost? Does it matter?
  2. Which skills should people keep even when a tool does them better? Who decides?
  3. In the lecture, students who practiced mathematics with a chatbot did better on the practice problems and worse on the exam without it. If you ran the course, what would you do?
  4. Who gains when a skill fades, and who bears the cost?

Discussion 4: the lecture’s topics

The lecture covered:

  • a short history, and AI as another level of abstraction in programming;
  • benchmarks: reliability and validity, hidden error, the best of several private versions, Goodhart’s law, verifiable domains;
  • what AI is built from: public writing, labeling work, Stack Overflow;
  • who benefits from it;
  • reliance, learning and deskilling;
  • conversational AI: sycophancy, medical advice, deepfakes and scams, political ads;
  • environmental costs;
  • a report with errors, and who signs.

Do you have any more questions or thoughts about any of these?

Discussion 5: you sign the report

You are an analyst. A tool chose the data cleaning steps and the model for an analysis, and wrote the first draft of the report. Your name goes on it.

  1. Alone, for a minute: what do you owe the reader before you sign?
  2. In groups: write a disclosure for the report, in at most three sentences.
  3. One group reads theirs. What could a reader still not know?
  4. Who is misled if the disclosure leaves that out?
